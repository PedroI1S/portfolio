10,935 m: A descent into the Challenger DeepDEEP

0 m · sea level 11°22′ N · 142°35′ E

Location: 11 degrees 22 minutes north, 142 degrees 35 minutes east, in the western Pacific.

The deepest place we know of lies at the southern end of the Mariana Trench, southwest of Guam. It is about 10,935 metres down. Scroll to sink there, one zone at a time.

010–200 m · 0–656 ft

Sunlight zone

Epipelagic

Water swallows sunlight colour by colour. Reds fade within the first few tens of metres, then yellows and greens, and blue reaches deepest. Nearly all of the ocean’s plant life grows up here, because below about 200 metres there is rarely enough light left to drive photosynthesis.

The light disappears

Twilight zone · Mesopelagic

A dim blue glow still reaches the twilight zone, far too faint for plants. Instruments can detect sunlight down to about 1,000 metres; past that line it is gone for good. Many animals that live here rise toward the surface at night to feed and sink back down before dawn.

Light made by animals

Midnight zone · Bathypelagic

From here down, the only light is made by living things. In 17 years of deep-sea video surveys off California, from the surface to 3,900 metres, about three in every four animals recorded could make their own light: to lure prey, to startle predators, to find each other in the dark.

Below the average

Abyss · Abyssopelagic

You have passed the ocean’s average depth, about 3,682 metres. Wide, flat plains of fine sediment lie at these depths across much of the world. The water sits just above freezing, the pressure is 400 to 600 times what it is at the surface, and most food arrives as marine snow: flakes of dead plankton and waste sinking slowly from above.

Into the trench

Hadal zone · Hadopelagic

Below 6,000 metres the ocean exists almost only inside trenches, where one tectonic plate dives beneath another. The zone takes its name from Hades, the Greek underworld. The Mariana Trench is more than 2,500 kilometres long and, on average, about 69 kilometres wide.

Trench walls

The Challenger Deep is a steep-walled valley cut into the floor of the trench, at its southern end.

Where fish give out

Hadal zone

Snailfish, small and translucent, have been caught in the Mariana Trench at almost 8,000 metres. Fish seem to run out at around 8,200 to 8,400 metres: the molecule that shields their proteins from pressure would have to build up beyond what their cells can balance. Life carries on without them. Shrimp-like amphipods scavenge all the way to the bottom.

07≈ 9,000 m · 23 January 1960

A crack at 9,000 metres

Bathyscaphe Trieste

On 23 January 1960 the bathyscaphe Trieste carried Jacques Piccard and U.S. Navy Lieutenant Don Walsh down into the Challenger Deep, two men in a steel sphere hung beneath a float of petrol. Somewhere past 9,000 metres an outer window pane cracked with a bang that shook the whole vessel. They kept going.

08 · The bottom of the Challenger Deep

10,935 m

± 6 m · about 35,876 ft · ≈ 1,090 atm · ≈ 1–4 °C · no sunlight

Trieste settled onto the floor after 4 hours and 47 minutes. Piccard and Walsh stayed for about 20 minutes, under roughly a thousand times the pressure at the surface, then spent more than three hours rising back to daylight.

Their instruments read 37,800 feet; the figure was later corrected to 10,916 metres. Modern surveys put the deepest point at about 10,935 metres, give or take a few. Stand Mount Everest, 8,849 metres tall, on the floor here and its summit would still be about two kilometres under water.

Nobody went back for 52 years.

Notes on the numbers

Pressure is estimated from depth (1 atm + ρgh, with seawater at about 1,030 kg/m³), so read it as approximate. Water temperatures are typical bands for the tropical western Pacific, rounded. The sunlight bar drops one step for every tenfold loss of light in clear ocean water. The scroll scale is stretched near the surface so each zone gets room, and it slows down where you pass the scattering layer and the Titanic so both drift by at true scale; the drawing above is to scale throughout.
