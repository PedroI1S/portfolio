Squares · Cover story · p. 18

A bench is a promise that you are allowed to stay.

Fig. 1 — Bench no. 17, under the planes, 7:40 a.m.
Photograph: Teodor Lindqvist

For twelve months our editor sat on every public bench in one neighbourhood, twenty minutes’ walk from end to end. There were forty-one of them. Nine are gone now.

Words by Inês Valadares · Photographs by Teodor Lindqvist

The first bench was outside the pharmacy, and it was already taken. A man in a cardigan was reading the same page of a sports paper that he would be reading, I later learned, most mornings until noon. He moved his bag without looking up. That small shuffle, the one that makes room for a stranger, is what this whole issue is about.

I started counting because of a rumour. Someone at the market said the benches were being taken away, quietly, one at a time, wherever a neighbour complained about noise. I wanted to know if it was true, so I bought a notebook and a cushion and began at the north end, sitting on each bench for at least an hour, in every season.

Most benches are placed by someone who will never sit on them. They are drawn on a plan as a small rectangle, ordered from a catalogue by the metre and bolted to a slab. The good ones face something: a fountain, a doorway, a game of cards. The bad ones face traffic, or a blank wall, or the sun at exactly the hour anyone would want to use them.

41benches on one street, counted in one year

Take away the bench and you haven’t removed the loiterer. You’ve removed the grandmother, the delivery rider and the man waiting for his bus.

Over the year, nine benches disappeared. Three became planters, two became bicycle racks and four became nothing at all: four pale rectangles of newer paving where the feet used to be. Each removal followed a complaint. As far as I could find, none of the complaints came from anyone who had sat there.

The people who use a bench most are the people with the fewest other places to be: the old, the very young, the tired, the early. A street that offers them nowhere to stop isn’t neutral. It is telling them, politely, to keep moving.

There is good news, and it is cheap. Three new benches went in this spring after a petition from the school gate. They face the playground, they have backs and armrests, and by the second week the arms were already worn pale where hands push up from them. That is the only review a bench ever needs.

How we counted

Every public bench on the long block, visited at least four times, once a season, for an hour or more. “Removed” means the fixings were still in the paving. Private café chairs don’t count, however comfortable.
